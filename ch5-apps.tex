\chapter{System-Level Applications and Network Architectures}\label{chap:apps} % (fold)

\section{Introduction: From the Chip to the Optical Network}\label{sec:ch5_intro}

Transitioning from the large-scale programmable photonic integrated circuit detailed in Chapter 4, 
the focus now shifts toward its functional application within modern optical communications. While 
the full-scale smart transceiver undergoes the final stages of heterogeneous packaging, validating 
its foundational optical concepts and exploring its topological impact on network architectures 
remain critical imperatives. Consequently, this chapter bridges the gap between hardware design and 
system-level implementation, projecting the capabilities of programmable photonics from fundamental 
component emulation up to massive core-network switching nodes.

Section \ref{sec:pol_diversity} establishes the experimental baseline by presenting the empirical 
characterization of the core polarization diversity subsystem. Utilizing a dedicated test structure 
that perfectly mirrors the functional path of the main transceiver—comprising edge couplers, 
polarization splitter and rotators, and a central programmable unit cell—this section validates the 
hardware's dynamic response. Through the application of variable optical delays, the experimental 
assessment demonstrates the subsystem's impact on standard, high-speed data traffic, explicitly 
evaluating local area network wavelength division multiplexing (LWDM)\cite{ieee_lwdm}, coarse 
wavelength division multiplexing (CWDM)\cite{itut_g694_2}, and datacenter reach 4-lane (DR4) 
\cite{ieee_dr4} communication standards.

Projecting these validated building blocks into complex network topologies, Section 
\ref{sec:switching_nodes} investigates the strategic role of the smart transceiver within 
next-generation optical switching nodes. Leveraging architectural frameworks derived from European 
collaborative projects, this theoretical analysis evaluates the insertion of the kilo-scale photonic 
mesh into both \textit{Route-and-Select} and \textit{Broadcast-and-Select} configurations, 
demonstrating its potential to revolutionize dynamic capacity allocation and spectral management.

Finally, Section \ref{sec:multicast} broadens the scope to massive optical circuit switches (OCS) 
to address the ultimate challenge of spatial scalability and point-to-multipoint signal distribution.
Building upon the spatial routing principles established by the hexagonal meshes in the previous 
chapter, a highly scalable $32 \times 32$ OCS operating in the O-band is evaluated. This study 
specifically explores high-efficiency multicasting capabilities, discussing the current physical 
limitations and the necessary architectural improvements to approach a fully non-blocking 
$1 \times 32$ optical distribution.

\section{Experimental Emulation of the Polarization Diversity Core}\label{sec:pol_diversity}

To empirically evaluate the impact of differential group delay within the polarization diversity 
scheme, a dedicated test structure was designed and fabricated. As depicted in Figure 
\ref{fig:ch5-pol_div_test_str}, the layout comprises four parallel variants of the core 
polarization subsystem. Each variant consists of an input edge coupler, a polarization 
splitter-rotator, a central programmable unit cell coupled with a phase shifter, a polarization 
rotator-combiner (PRC), and an output EC. The fundamental difference among the four structures 
lies in the engineered on-chip delay lines introduced immediately after the PSR. Specifically, the 
path length differences ($\Delta L$) between the upper and lower arms are set to 0 µm, 700 µm, 
3500 µm, and 7000 µm. These physical length variations introduce approximate temporal delays of 
0 ps, 10 ps, 50 ps, and 100 ps, respectively, providing a systematic framework to assess 
delay-induced signal degradation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5-pol_div_test_str.png} 
    \caption{Layout of the polarization diversity test structures. The diagram highlights four 
    parallel evaluation paths (0 to 3), each featuring an input EC, a PSR, variable on-chip delay 
    lines, a central PUC/PS stage, a PRC, and an output EC.}
    \label{fig:ch5-pol_div_test_str}
\end{figure}


The experimental testbed was designed to emulate realistic, high-capacity network environments. 
Data generation was driven by a bit error rate tester (BERT) equipped with commercial 400GE 
transceiver modules. To cover a comprehensive range of standard data streams, the evaluation 
utilized co-polarized 400GE-LWDM, both co-polarized and non-co-polarized 400GE-CWDM, and DR4 
transmitters. To rigorously test the hardware's polarization handling capabilities, a polarization 
controller was inserted at the output of the transmitter. Prior to coupling the signal into the 
photonic integrated circuit, a polarimeter was employed to verify and calibrate the input state of 
polarization (SOP). This precise pre-calibration enabled systematic measurements under three 
distinct injection scenarios: 100\% TE, 100\% TM, and a perfectly balanced 50-50\% TE-TM mixed 
state.

Physical light coupling was achieved using cleaved optical fibers aligned to the on-chip edge 
couplers. The alignment process was optimized on an optical table equipped with piezo-controlled 
micro-positioners, utilizing an optical power meter (OPM) to provide real-time feedback and 
maximize insertion efficiency. To guarantee stable operating conditions and avoid thermal drift 
during the measurements, a thermo-electric cooler (TEC) was used to firmly fix the chip 
temperature at 45°C. Finally, the active components of the test structure—namely the PUC and the 
phase shifters—were electrically driven via a multi-contact probe. Two independent current sources 
were employed to feed the upper and lower arms individually, ensuring highly precise and decoupled 
tuning of the subsystem.

To compensate for the inherent insertion losses of the test structure and the fiber-to-chip 
coupling interfaces, a semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) was placed at the output of the PIC. 
This stage boosts the optical signal to an optimal power level before it is fed back into the BERT 
receiver for error analysis. A comprehensive view of the experimental testbed is presented in 
Figure \ref{fig:ch5-pol_div_setup}, where a photograph of the laboratory environment is overlaid 
with the logical block diagram of the interconnected instruments.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Static Spectral Characterization}
\label{subsec:spectral_char}

Before assessing the dynamic performance with high-speed data streams, an initial static 
characterization was conducted to evaluate the continuous-wave (CW) optical response of the test 
structures. A tunable laser source was swept across the entire O-band, covering the wavelength 
range from 1260~nm to 1360~nm. This broad spectral sweep is essential to ensure that the baseline 
insertion losses and the wavelength-dependent behavior of the components (ECs, PSR, PUC, and PRC) 
are compatible with the wide grid of CWDM and the fine grid of LWDM standards.

The spectral responses were recorded for the four parallel structures, where $\Delta L = (0, 700, 
\allowbreak 3500, 7000)~\mu\mathrm{m}$ under the three pre-calibrated injection states. Firstly, 
each structure requires electrical tuning of the phase shifters to set the PUCs in their 
transmission maxima (specifically, for this test structure, the PUCs are configured in BAR state). 
The spectral results are compiled in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-pol_div_spectra}. For the purely 
co-polarized injection states, namely 100\% TE (a) and 100\% TM (b), the input light is 
predominantly routed through a single arm of the diversity circuit. Consequently, the transmission 
spectra primarily reflect the baseline insertion losses of the active optical path.
Conversely, the 50-50\% TE-TM mixed state (c) inherently excites both the upper and lower arms of 
the polarization subsystem simultaneously.  As expected, the optical power measured at the output
of each structure is very similar either using pure TE, pure TM or a 50-50 mixed state. This result
not only validates the structure but also reports low polarization-dependent loss (PDL). Note that
there is a noticeable ripple in all the traces independent of the SOP. This behavior is 
characteristic of a Fabry-Pérot cavity effect, caused by reflections at the chip facets when 
coupling cleaved fibers directly to the ECs. As the performance is predictable and stable for each 
wavelength, it does not imply any impairment in the transmission measurements discussed in the next 
section.

    \caption{Static spectral characterization across the O-band (1260--1360~nm) of the four test 
    structures where $\Delta L = (0, 700, 3500, 7000)~\mu\mathrm{m}$. Transmission spectra are 
    shown for (a) pure TE injection, (b) pure TM injection, and (c) 50-50\% TE-TM mixed state.}
    \label{fig:ch5-pol_div_spectra}
\end{figure}

To further validate the polarization-agnostic nature of the subsystem, an evaluation of the 
polarization-dependent loss was conducted on the matched-length structure 
($\Delta L = 0~\mu\mathrm{m}$). By systematically varying the input state of polarization across 
multiple azimuth ($30^\circ, 45^\circ$, and $60^\circ$) and ellipticity 
($0^\circ, 15^\circ, 30^\circ$, and $45^\circ$) configurations, the optical response was recorded 
across the entire O-band. Note that changing the state of polarization implies decoupling the 
light to the chip, measuring with the polarimeter and coupling again to the PIC facet. Despite
using very precise equipment in the optical coupling stations, minimum power variations can be 
present (typically below 0.1 dBm).

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-pdl_grouped}, the transmission spectra 
exhibit minimal variance regardless of the input polarization state. The maximum recorded PDL 
remained tightly bounded below 2.09~dB, with an average value of 1.15~dB across the measured 
spectrum. This minimal fluctuation demonstrates robust resilience against the random polarization 
drifts typically encountered in deployed single-mode fiber links, confirming the excellent 
performance of the integrated polarization diversity scheme.

\subsection{Dynamic Performance at 400G}\label{subsec:dynamic_perf}

Following the static validation, the dynamic transmission performance of the polarization diversity 
subsystem was evaluated using standard 400G data streams. The bit error rate (BER) was measured 
before any forward error correction (pre-FEC) was applied. To evaluate the resilience of the system 
against differential group delay (DGD), the measurements were carried out across the four path 
length differences $\Delta L = (0, 700, 3500, 7000)~\mu\mathrm{m}$ using four distinct 
transmitter configurations: co-polarized LWDM, co-polarized CWDM, non-co-polarized CWDM (no-COP), 
and DR4. The measured pre-FEC BER values are presented in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-ber_results}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/ch5-ber_results.png}
    \caption{Dynamic transmission results showing the pre-FEC BER as a function of the added path 
    length difference ($\Delta L$). The evaluation covers LWDM, CWDM, non-co-polarized CWDM, and 
    DR4 formats.}
    \label{fig:ch5-ber_results}
\end{figure}

It is worth noting that for the DR4 transceiver, the measurements were performed using only one of 
its four spatial lanes. Unlike LWDM and CWDM, which multiplex four wavelengths into a single 
optical fiber, DR4 relies on parallel optics, utilizing four independent fibers that each carry a 
100G signal at 1310~nm. Given the single-input/single-output architecture of the photonic test 
structure, evaluating a single DR4 lane is mechanically necessary and fully representative of the 
standard's performance under DGD constraints.

For the strictly co-polarized injection states (pure TE and pure TM), the pre-FEC BER remains well 
below the standard KP4 FEC threshold (typically around $2.4 \times 10^{-4}$) 
\cite{ieee_802_3ck-2022} for all tested delay lines and transceiver formats. In these scenarios, 
the PSR routes almost the entirety of the optical power through a single physical arm. 
Consequently, the signal does not experience any multipath interference upon recombination at the 
PRC, making the transmission completely immune to the physical delay engineered in the parallel 
empty arm.

However, a critical degradation is observed for the 50-50\% TE-TM mixed state and the CWDM no-COP 
scenario. In these cases, the optical power is split between the upper and lower arms. 
At $\Delta L = 0~\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $\Delta L = 700~\mu\mathrm{m}$ (representing approximately 0 
and 10~ps of delay, respectively), the pre-FEC BER remains stable below the threshold, thus the 
FEC done by RS(544, 514) decoding ensures error-free transmission. This indicates that the internal 
digital signal processing (DSP) equalizers of the commercial 400G receivers are capable of 
successfully compensating for up to $\sim 10$~ps of temporal skew between the polarization 
branches.

Conversely, when the path length difference is increased to $3500~\mu\mathrm{m}$ 
($\sim 50$~ps) and $7000~\mu\mathrm{m}$ ($\sim 101$~ps), the link completely drops, marked as 
``FAIL'' in the corresponding plots. State-of-the-art 400G Ethernet relies on 53-Gbaud PAM4 
modulation, yielding a symbol duration of approximately 18.8~ps. A temporal skew of 50 or 100~ps 
means the delayed copy of the signal shifts by several symbol periods, causing severe inter-symbol 
interference (ISI) that vastly exceeds the equalization tap limits of the receiver DSP. This 
catastrophic failure is equally reflected in the CWDM no-COP measurements, as the independent and 
random polarization of each wavelength inherently forces power splitting across both delayed arms.

Ultimately, these dynamic results demonstrate that while the proposed polarization diversity scheme 
introduces negligible penalty under matched-length conditions, strict path length balancing is 
mandatory. To guarantee error-free 400G operation, the structural length difference between the 
active and passive arms must be carefully minimized to keep the temporal skew well within the 
receiver's equalization capabilities.

\section{Smart Transceiver in Next-Generation Switching Nodes}
\label{sec:switching_nodes}

\let\thefootnote\relax\footnotetext{\textit{The author would like to gratefully acknowledge 
Dr. José Manuel Rivas-Moscoso - Telefónica for providing the conceptual schematics of the switching 
node architectures discussed in this section.}}

As optical networks scale to accommodate the exponential growth in data traffic, the flexibility 
and reconfigurability of the routing elements become paramount. While the previous sections 
validated the performance of the proposed structures in point-to-point (P2P) links, modern optical 
networks rely heavily on dynamically reconfigurable optical switching nodes to manage traffic 
across meshed topologies. In this context, the integration of smart transceivers—capable of 
adapting to varying network conditions, routing paths, and signal formats—provides a significant 
advantage for next-generation architectures \cite{bhat6GEcosystemCurrent2021, 
welchPointtoMultipointOpticalNetworks2021}.

Optical switching nodes, often implemented as reconfigurable optical add-drop multiplexers 
(ROADMs), are typically based on two predominant internal architectures: route-and-select (R\&S) 
and broadcast-and-select (B\&S). 

In a route-and-select architecture, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-route_and_select}, active 
switching elements are utilized at both the ingress and egress stages. For standard express 
traffic, an input WSS dynamically routes the incoming wavelengths directly to their intended output 
WSS, which then multiplexes them into the outgoing fiber. The R\&S paradigm minimizes the insertion 
losses associated with passive splitting, offering superior scalability and enhanced channel 
isolation.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5-route_and_select.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of a route-and-select (R\&S) optical switching node. 
    Courtesy of Dr. José Manuel Rivas-Moscoso - Telefónica}
    \label{fig:ch5-route_and_select}
\end{figure}

In addition to point-to-point express routing, these nodes must support advanced multicast 
capabilities, such as the drop-and-continue (D\&C) functionality. D\&C allows a specific wavelength 
to be split within the node so that a fraction of the optical power is dropped locally to a 
receiver, while the remaining signal continues its path through the egress WSS toward subsequent 
nodes in the network. To enable this, the affected signals are diverted through an internal local 
mesh (typically comprising optical splitters or multicast switches).

However, as indicated in the architectural schematics, express connections that do not require D\&C 
functionality can completely skip this internal mesh, traveling directly from the input WSS to the 
output WSS to minimize insertion losses and save internal resources. Moreover, to provide further 
routing flexibility and enable contentionless add/drop capabilities, more than one connection can 
be established between each WSS and the local mesh (if there are enough ports available).


Conversely, in a broadcast-and-select architecture, such as the one depicted in Figure 
\ref{fig:ch5-broadcast_and_select}, incoming optical signals are passively split and distributed 
(broadcast) to all possible output directions using optical power splitters. In the architecture 
presented in the figure, a distinctive feature is that the splitting is done exclusively by the 
programmable PIC core. At each output port, a wavelength selective switch (WSS) or a tunable filter 
is employed to select the specific wavelengths destined for that path, blocking all others. While 
B\&S is cost-effective and straightforward to implement for nodes with a low degree of connectivity, 
the inherent splitting losses scale poorly as the number of ports increases.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5-broadcast_and_select.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of a broadcast-and-select (B\&S) optical switching node. 
    Courtesy of Dr. José Manuel Rivas-Moscoso - Telefónica. Note: PSR not shown for simplicity, but 
    two IO ports are required in the smart transceiver.}
    \label{fig:ch5-broadcast_and_select}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Smart Transceiver for Route \& Select Architecture: Performance Estimation and 
Limitations}

In a route-and-select architecture, the smart transceiver operates by dynamically routing signals 
to and from the local mesh or directly interfacing with the corresponding wavelength selective 
switches (WSSs). To evaluate the feasibility of deploying the proposed programmable PIC core 
within this architecture, a comprehensive scalability and power budget analysis was conducted. 

The overall insertion loss of the smart transceiver is highly dependent on the physical length and 
complexity of the configured optical circuit within the hexagonal mesh. According to the estimated 
performance metrics, the baseline penalty includes an edge coupling loss (in/out) ranging from 2.5 
to 5~dB, combined with a static routing loss of 4 to 6~dB. The total attenuation is then determined 
by the number of traversed PUCs and the intrinsic 3~dB penalty introduced by every $1\times 2$ 
optical split. Notice that, apart from crossing the mesh cells, there is an extra PUC in the MUA
that should be accounted for.

Figure \ref{fig:ch5-RS_mesh} illustrates several routing examples mapped onto the 
PIC. For instance, a short routing path spanning just 3 PUCs (e.g., the magenta path connecting 
adjacent ports) introduces a total loss between 11.1 and 16.4~dB. Conversely, a highly complex path 
crossing 35 PUCs from one end of the chip to the other (e.g., the purple path) can scale the losses 
up to 23.9 -- 35.6~dB. A detailed breakdown of these path-dependent insertion losses is provided in 
Table \ref{tab:ch5-rs_path_losses}.

\subsection{Smart Transceiver for Broadcast \& Select Architecture: Performance Estimation and 
Limitations}

In the broadcast-and-select architecture, the power budget constraints are primarily dictated by 
the optical power splitting required to distribute the signal. As highlighted in the architectural 
schematics, a distinctive advantage of integrating the proposed smart transceiver is that the 
optical splitting required for the broadcast operation can be performed exclusively by the 
programmable PIC core, eliminating the need for external passive splitters. 

To evaluate this capability, the internal hexagonal waveguide mesh of the transceiver was 
configured to route and split the optical signal into multiple paths simultaneously, as depicted in 
Figure \ref{fig:ch5-BS_mesh}. The mesh can dynamically form complex $1\times N$ splitting trees to 
serve both the local add/drop ports and the express directions toward adjacent network nodes.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/ch5-BS_mesh.png}
    \caption{Routing and splitting configurations within the programmable PIC core. The hexagonal

    splitters (e.g., $1\times 2$ or $1\times 6$), distributing the input signal across multiple 
    output ports for B\&S operation.}

\end{figure}

ideal conditions, every $1\times 2$ optical split intrinsically reduces the signal power by at 
least 3~dB. This intrinsic splitting penalty is further compounded by the physical insertion and 
propagation losses of the PIC. Applying the same baseline metrics defined in the previous 
section—including fiber-to-chip coupling, static routing, and individual PUC insertion losses—the 
total attenuation for complex broadcast trees can be accurately estimated.

the available optical power budget directly limits the maximum connectivity. The analysis

splitting ratio, the mesh possesses sufficient spatial capacity to allocate between 4 and 16 
simultaneous optical circuits without spatial contention.

It is worth noting that while spatial contention is strictly avoided in the presented routing 
configurations, traversing multiple internal components and waveguide crossings inevitably 
introduces a certain degree of optical crosstalk. However, the foundational building blocks of the 
programmable mesh, particularly the waveguide crossings, are typically designed to exhibit high 
optical isolation. Therefore, for the scope of this scalability and power budget analysis, the 
accumulated crosstalk is assumed to remain within acceptable system-level penalty margins, 
preserving the required signal integrity for the simultaneous optical circuits.






\section{Scaling up Routing Capabilities: Multicasting in O-Band OCS}\label{sec:multicast}

\begin{center}
    \textit{The results and figures presented in this section have been published in: 
    A. Santomé-Valverde \textit{et al.}, "Programmable 1xN Multicast for 400G Data Center Networks 
    via a 32x32 SDN-Controlled Silicon Photonics Optical Circuit Switch," \textit{IEEE Summer 
    Topicals Meeting Series (SUM)}, 2026 \cite{Santome2026_SummerTopicals}. \copyright~2026 IEEE.}
\end{center}
\vspace{1em}

While the previous sections have explored various routing capabilities and test structures—ranging 
from the polarization diversity characterizations in Section \ref{sec:pol_diversity} to the complex 
ROADM architectures tailored for telecom networks in Section \ref{sec:switching_nodes}—modern 
optical networking faces equally critical bottlenecks at a different scale: within the data centers 
themselves. The exponential growth of cloud computing, data-intensive applications, and 
particularly artificial intelligence (AI) workloads has fundamentally transformed intra-data center 
traffic \cite{patronasOpticalSwitchingData2025, poutievskiJupiterEvolvingTransforming2022}.


Traditional hierarchical switching architectures, primarily designed for north-south traffic, are 
struggling to efficiently manage the massive surge of modern east-west inter-server communications 
\cite{satoOpticalSwitchingWill2024}. To address this, OCS implemented via silicon photonics (SiPh) 
are being rapidly adopted to provide high-bandwidth, low-latency, and energy-efficient 
reconfigurable fabrics. In this AI-centric context, multicast communication emerges as a critical 
networking primitive. Multicasting allows a single data stream to be delivered simultaneously to 
multiple recipients, significantly increasing bandwidth efficiency and reducing transmission 
latency compared to multiple unicast streams. 

Despite its tremendous potential for distributed AI training and database replication, optical 
multicast has historically seen limited adoption in large-scale data centers due to severe 
scalability concerns and the lack of flexible support in conventional electronic switching 
hardware \cite{komolafeIPMulticastVirtualized2017, 
rizzelliAnalysisExperimentalDemonstration2025}. The intrinsic power penalty associated with 
splitting an optical signal to multiple ports typically degrades the signal-to-noise ratio beyond 
acceptable limits for high-speed datacom transceivers.


To overcome these physical limitations, this section shifts the experimental focus to a different 
hardware platform explicitly tailored for the datacom regime. Expanding upon the earlier O-band 
evaluations, the experimental validation presented here utilizes a highly scalable $32\times32$ 
SDN-controlled SiPh OCS to route and split 400G LAN-WDM traffic. By leveraging the flexible 
routing of this $32\times32$ mesh in combination with the intra-system gain compensation provided 
by external SOAs incorporated within the OCS module, the inherent splitting losses of the multicast 
tree are effectively mitigated, enabling error-free point-to-multipoint (P2MP) transmissions for 
next-generation Ethernet standards.


\subsection{Experimental Setup for 400G WDM Multicast}
\label{subsec:multicast_setup}

A 400G WDM testbed was deployed to experimentally validate the multicasting capabilities of the 
programmable optical circuit switch in a datacom scenario. Figure \ref{fig:ch5-multicast_setup} 
illustrates the complete experimental setup used for this demonstration.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/ch5-multicast_setup.png} 
    \caption{SDN-controlled $32\times32$ SiPh OCS experimental setup for 400G WDM multicast 
    ($1\times4$ configuration) using gNMI. \textit{Reproduced from 
    \cite{Santome2026_SummerTopicals}. \copyright~2026 IEEE.}}
    \label{fig:ch5-multicast_setup}
\end{figure}

At the transmitter side, a commercial 400G quad small form factor pluggable double density 
(QSFP-DD) LAN-WDM transceiver acts as the optical source, generating four 100G PAM4 modulated 
wavelengths operating in the O-band. With a total optical power budget of 12.5~dB, this module 
requires a minimum loss suppression of 5~dB to prevent receiver saturation. Once generated, the 
multi-wavelength signal is injected into input port 18 of the $32\times32$ SiPh OCS.

Within the core switching fabric, light is routed through a mesh of programmable unit cells based 
on $2\times2$ MZIs. Unlike traditional passive splitters, the programmable PIC works in tandem
with external SOAs, building a gain-controlled OCS system. These active components provide 
the necessary gain compensation to mitigate the splitting losses inherent to the multicast 
operation. 

To enable the point-to-multipoint (P2MP) multicast operation, an SDN controller dynamically 
configures the PUCs in tunable-state (TS) via the gNMI protocol by adjusting their arm-loaded phase 
shifters. This configuration allows the input signal to be accurately split across multiple output 
ports simultaneously.

For characterization and signal integrity assessment, the multicast outputs are routed to two 
primary instruments. First, an optical spectrum analyzer (OSA) handles spectral verification and 
optical signal-to-noise ratio (OSNR) monitoring. Simultaneously, a BER tester is employed for 
transmission quality analysis. In order to validate the system performance against the IEEE 
802.3ck-2022 standard for 400 Gb/s Ethernet, the measurements must fall below the KP4 FEC 
threshold (pre-FEC $BER \le 2.4 \times 10^{-4}$) to guarantee error-free data recovery. 
Theoretically, the expected BER for PAM4 signals operating over additive white Gaussian noise 
(AWGN) channels can be estimated as:

\begin{equation}
    BER_{PAM4} \approx \frac{3}{8} \cdot \text{erfc}\left(\sqrt{\frac{SNR}{5}}\right)
\end{equation}

As dictated by the physical principles discussed in Section \ref{sec:switching_nodes}, increasing 
the multicast radix inherently introduces a continuous optical splitting penalty. Counteracting 
this structural attenuation requires the external SOAs within the OCS module to provide 
progressively higher gain. Pushing the amplifiers closer to their saturation limit inevitably 
introduces optical noise and non-linearities, which fundamentally explains the stepped BER 
degradation observed in the chart. 

Specifically, transitioning to the $1\times3$ configuration raises the average pre-FEC BER to 
$3.49 \times 10^{-5}$. Maximizing the splitting ratio of the current testbed to a $1\times4$ radix 
further increases the average BER to $1.22 \times 10^{-4}$. Despite this expected physical 
degradation, the crucial takeaway from Figure \ref{fig:ch5-BER_multicast} is that all measured 
ports across every configuration remain strictly below the standard KP4 FEC threshold. By 
preserving a robust operational margin, the system guarantees error-free signal recovery, 
successfully proving the viability of programmable silicon photonics for 400G point-to-multipoint 
datacenter architectures.


\subsubsection{Power Budget Constraints and Scalability towards $1\times32$}

Despite the successful experimental demonstrations up to a $1\times4$ radix, scaling the system 
toward a fully non-blocking $1\times32$ multicast tree remains constrained by current power budget 
limitations. Primarily bounded by the SOA saturation regime and the passive insertion losses of the 
silicon switching fabric, the maximum feasible P2MP distribution is currently capped. 

Operating in the fundamental $1\times1$ configuration, the OCS presents a baseline insertion loss 
of 12~dB. Given an input transceiver power of 6~dBm and a target output power of 2~dBm, the SOA is 
required to provide 8~dB of gain. Scaling the routing tree to a $1\times4$ radix adds a 6~dB 
optical splitting loss, inherently pushing the required SOA gain to 14~dB. Hitting the device's 
exact saturation limit, this demanding operating point introduces the optical noise and 
non-linearities responsible for the stepped BER degradation observed in Figure 
\ref{fig:ch5-BER_multicast}.

Consequently, enabling a massive $1\times32$ multicast split necessitates targeted hardware 
optimizations. Intrinsically adding a 15~dB optical penalty, this extreme splitting ratio requires 
careful budget management. Considering the current transceiver sensitivity of -12.1~dBm, 
error-free $1\times32$ routing demands a minimum output power of -3~dBm. Achieving this signal 
integrity within the strict linear regime of the amplifiers will require future iterations of the 
OCS module to reduce intrinsic baseline losses to approximately 6~dB—potentially through optimized 
ultra-low-loss splitters \cite{papadovasilakisFabricationTolerantOBandWDM2024} and advanced edge 
couplers—while simultaneously enhancing the noise figure of the integrated gain blocks. 

Fulfilling these optimized optical conditions would allow the $32\times32$ SiPh OCS to fully 
support massive high-radix multicasting. Overcoming these physical barriers will ultimately 
consolidate programmable photonics as a definitive solution to meet the ultra-high bandwidth and 
low-latency demands of next-generation AI-driven data centers.

\section{Chapter Summary and Outlook}
\label{sec:ch5_conclusions}

Bridging the critical gap between integrated photonic design and system-level implementation, this 
chapter has successfully projected the capabilities of programmable optical circuits from 
foundational hardware emulation up to massive core-network switching architectures. 

Validating the fundamental physical layer, the experimental characterization of the polarization 
diversity subsystem demonstrated robust compatibility with standard 400G communication formats. 
Under matched-length conditions, the subsystem exhibits minimal polarization-dependent loss, 
ensuring highly reliable signal recovery regardless of the input state of polarization. However, 
the dynamic evaluation highlighted strict path length balancing as an absolute design imperative. 
Because uncompensated temporal skews exceeding 50~ps introduce severe inter-symbol interference 
that vastly surpasses the equalization limits of commercial receiver DSPs, minimizing differential 
group delay is mandatory for future transceiver iterations.

Projecting these validated building blocks onto metro and core telecom networks, theoretical 
evaluation confirmed the strategic value of the smart transceiver within modern ROADM topologies. 
By dynamically managing spatial routing and internal power splitting, the programmable hexagonal 
waveguide mesh can effectively absorb the complexity of both Route-and-Select and 
Broadcast-and-Select nodes. This high-density integration provides collision-free add/drop 
multiplexing and flexible capacity allocation, significantly reducing the reliance on bulky 
external hardware elements. 

Addressing the explosive bandwidth demands of modern intra-data center traffic, the final 
experimental campaign successfully scaled the routing paradigm to a $32\times32$ SDN-controlled 
silicon photonics OCS. Engineered specifically for the datacom regime, this active switching 
fabric enabled software-defined point-to-multipoint routing, achieving error-free 400G LAN-WDM 
multicasting up to a $1\times4$ radix. Although current power budget constraints—primarily bounded 
by the saturation threshold of the integrated SOAs—temporarily cap the maximum splitting ratio, 
quantifying these exact limitations provides a clear technological roadmap. Implementing targeted 
loss-reduction strategies in future chip generations will decisively unlock fully non-blocking 
$1\times32$ multicast capabilities.

Ultimately, the comprehensive evaluations presented throughout this chapter confirm the 
transformative potential of programmable photonics. Proving highly versatile across both C-band 
telecom infrastructures and O-band AI-driven datacenters, intelligent optical switching emerges as 
a cornerstone technology to meet the ultra-high bandwidth, low-latency, and dynamic 
reconfigurability demands of next-generation optical networks.
