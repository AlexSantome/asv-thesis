\chapter{System-Level Applications and Network Architectures}\label{chap:apps} % (fold)

\section{Introduction: From the Chip to the Optical Network}\label{sec:ch5_intro}

Transitioning from the kilo-scale programmable photonic integrated circuit detailed in Chapter 4, 
the focus now shifts toward its functional application within modern optical communications. While 
the full-scale smart transceiver undergoes the final stages of heterogeneous packaging, validating 
its foundational optical concepts and exploring its topological impact on network architectures 
remain critical imperatives. Consequently, this chapter bridges the gap between hardware design and 
system-level implementation, projecting the capabilities of programmable photonics from fundamental 
component emulation up to massive core-network switching nodes.

Section \ref{sec:pol_diversity} establishes the experimental baseline by presenting the empirical 
characterization of the core polarization diversity subsystem. Utilizing a dedicated test structure 
that perfectly mirrors the functional path of the main transceiver—comprising edge couplers, 
polarization splitter and rotators, and a central programmable unit cell—this section validates the 
hardware's dynamic response. Through the application of variable optical delays, the experimental 
assessment demonstrates the subsystem's impact on standard, high-speed data traffic, explicitly 
evaluating local area network wavelength division multiplexing (LWDM)\cite{ieee_lwdm}, coarse 
wavelength division multiplexing (CWDM)\cite{itut_g694_2}, and datacenter reach 4-lane (DR4) 
\cite{ieee_dr4} communication standards.

Projecting these validated building blocks into complex network topologies, Section 
\ref{sec:switching_nodes} investigates the strategic role of the smart transceiver within 
next-generation optical switching nodes. Leveraging architectural frameworks derived from European 
collaborative projects, this theoretical analysis evaluates the insertion of the kilo-scale photonic 
mesh into both \textit{Route-and-Select} and \textit{Broadcast-and-Select} configurations, 
demonstrating its potential to revolutionize dynamic capacity allocation and spectral management.

Finally, Section \ref{sec:multicast} broadens the scope to massive optical circuit switches (OCS) 
to address the ultimate challenge of spatial scalability and point-to-multipoint signal distribution.
Building upon the spatial routing principles established by the hexagonal meshes in the previous 
chapter, a highly scalable $32 \times 32$ OCS operating in the O-band is evaluated. This study 
specifically explores high-efficiency multicasting capabilities, discussing the current physical 
limitations and the necessary architectural improvements to approach a fully non-blocking 
$1 \times 32$ optical distribution.

\section{Experimental Emulation of the Polarization Diversity Core}\label{sec:pol_diversity}

To empirically evaluate the impact of differential group delay within the polarization diversity 
scheme, a dedicated test structure was designed and fabricated. As depicted in Figure 
\ref{fig:ch5-pol_div_test_str}, the layout comprises four parallel variants of the core 
polarization subsystem. Each variant consists of an input edge coupler, a polarization 
splitter-rotator, a central programmable unit cell coupled with a phase shifter , a polarization 
rotator-combiner (PRC), and an output EC. The fundamental difference among the four structures 
lies in the engineered on-chip delay lines introduced immediately after the PSR. Specifically, the 
path length differences ($\Delta L$) between the upper and lower arms are set to 0 µm, 700 µm, 
3500 µm, and 7000 µm. These physical length variations introduce approximate temporal delays of 
0 ps, 10 ps, 50 ps, and 100 ps, respectively, providing a systematic framework to assess 
delay-induced signal degradation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5-pol_div_test_str.png} 
    \caption{Layout of the polarization diversity test structures. The diagram highlights four 
    parallel evaluation paths (0 to 3), each featuring an input EC, a PSR, variable on-chip delay 
    lines, a central PUC/PS stage, a PRC, and an output EC.}
    \label{fig:ch5-pol_div_test_str}
\end{figure}


The experimental testbed was designed to emulate realistic, high-capacity network environments. 
Data generation was driven by a bit error rate tester (BERT) equipped with commercial 400GE 
transceiver modules. To cover a comprehensive range of standard data streams, the evaluation 
utilized co-polarized 400GE-LWDM, both co-polarized and non-co-polarized 400GE-CWDM, and DR4 
transmitters. To rigorously test the hardware's polarization handling capabilities, a polarization 
controller was inserted at the output of the transmitter. Prior to coupling the signal into the 
photonic integrated circuit, a polarimeter was employed to verify and calibrate the input state of 
polarization (SOP). This precise pre-calibration enabled systematic measurements under three 
distinct injection scenarios: 100\% TE, 100\% TM, and a perfectly balanced 50-50\% TE-TM mixed 
state.

Physical light coupling was achieved using cleaved optical fibers aligned to the on-chip edge 
couplers. The alignment process was optimized on an optical table equipped with piezo-controlled 
micro-positioners, utilizing an optical power meter (OPM) to provide real-time feedback and 
maximize insertion efficiency. To guarantee stable operating conditions and avoid thermal drift 
during the measurements, a thermo-electric cooler (TEC) was used to firmly fix the chip 
temperature at 45°C. Finally, the active components of the test structure—namely the PUC and the 
phase shifters—were electrically driven via a multi-contact probe. Two independent current sources 
were employed to feed the upper and lower arms individually, ensuring highly precise and decoupled 
tuning of the subsystem.

To compensate for the inherent insertion losses of the test structure and the fiber-to-chip 
coupling interfaces, a semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) was placed at the output of the PIC. 
This stage boosts the optical signal to an optimal power level before it is fed back into the BERT 
receiver for error analysis. A comprehensive view of the experimental testbed is presented in 
Figure \ref{fig:ch5-pol_div_setup}, where a photograph of the laboratory environment is overlaid 
with the logical block diagram of the interconnected instruments.

    Yellow boxes: key components in the signal path. Green boxes: standalone supporting modules.}
    \label{fig:ch5-pol_div_setup}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Static Spectral Characterization}
\label{subsec:spectral_char}

Before assessing the dynamic performance with high-speed data streams, an initial static 
characterization was conducted to evaluate the continuous-wave (CW) optical response of the test 
structures. A tunable laser source was swept across the entire O-band, covering the wavelength 
range from 1260~nm to 1360~nm. This broad spectral sweep is essential to ensure that the baseline 
insertion losses and the wavelength-dependent behavior of the components (ECs, PSR, PUC, and PRC) 
are compatible with the wide grid of CWDM and the fine grid of LWDM standards.

The spectral responses were recorded for the four parallel structures, where $\Delta L = (0, 700, 
\allowbreak 3500, 7000)~\mu\mathrm{m}$ under the three pre-calibrated injection states. Firstly, 
each structure requires electrical tuning of the phase shifters to set the PUCs in their 
transmission maxima (specifically, for this test structure, the PUCs are configured in BAR state). 
The spectral results are compiled in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-pol_div_spectra}. For the purely 
co-polarized injection states, namely 100\% TE (a) and 100\% TM (b), the input light is 
predominantly routed through a single arm of the diversity circuit. Consequently, the transmission 
spectra primarily reflect the baseline insertion losses of the active optical path.
Conversely, the 50-50\% TE-TM mixed state (c) inherently excites both the upper and lower arms of 
the polarization subsystem simultaneously.  As expected, the optical power measured at the output
of each structure is very similar either using pure TE, pure TM or a 50-50 mixed state. This result
not only validates the structure but also reports low polarization-dependent loss (PDL). Note that
there is a noticeable ripple in all the traces independent of the SOP. This behavior is 
characteristic of a Fabry-Pérot cavity effect, caused by reflections at the chip facets when 
coupling cleaved fibers directly to the ECs. As the performance is predictable and stable for each 
wavelength, it does not imply any impairment in the transmission measurements discussed in the next 
section.

    \caption{Static spectral characterization across the O-band (1260--1360~nm) of the four test 
    structures where $\Delta L = (0, 700, 3500, 7000)~\mu\mathrm{m}$. Transmission spectra are 
    shown for (a) pure TE injection, (b) pure TM injection, and (c) 50-50\% TE-TM mixed state.}
    \label{fig:ch5-pol_div_spectra}
\end{figure}

To further validate the polarization-agnostic nature of the subsystem, an evaluation of the 
polarization-dependent loss was conducted on the matched-length structure 
($\Delta L = 0~\mu\mathrm{m}$). By systematically varying the input state of polarization across 
multiple azimuth ($30^\circ, 45^\circ$, and $60^\circ$) and ellipticity 
($0^\circ, 15^\circ, 30^\circ$, and $45^\circ$) configurations, the optical response was recorded 
across the entire O-band. Note that changing the state of polarization implies decoupling the 
light to the chip, measuring with the polarimeter and coupling again to the PIC facet. Despite
using very precise equipment in the optical coupling stations, minimum power variations can be 
present (typically below 0.1 dBm).

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-pdl_grouped}, the transmission spectra 
exhibit minimal variance regardless of the input polarization state. The maximum recorded PDL 
remained tightly bounded below 2.09~dB, with an average value of 1.15~dB across the measured 
spectrum. This minimal fluctuation demonstrates robust resilience against the random polarization 
drifts typically encountered in deployed single-mode fiber links, confirming the excellent 
performance of the integrated polarization diversity scheme.

\subsection{Dynamic Performance at 400G}\label{subsec:dynamic_perf}

Following the static validation, the dynamic transmission performance of the polarization diversity 
subsystem was evaluated using standard 400G data streams. The bit error rate (BER) was measured 
before any forward error correction (pre-FEC) was applied. To evaluate the resilience of the system 
against differential group delay (DGD), the measurements were carried out across the four path 
length differences $\Delta L = (0, 700, 3500, 7000)~\mu\mathrm{m}$ using four distinct 
transmitter configurations: co-polarized LWDM, co-polarized CWDM, non-co-polarized CWDM (no-COP), 
and DR4. The measured pre-FEC BER values are presented in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-ber_results}.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/ch5-ber_results.png}
    \caption{Dynamic transmission results showing the pre-FEC BER as a function of the added path 
    length difference ($\Delta L$). The evaluation covers LWDM, CWDM, non-co-polarized CWDM, and 
    DR4 formats.}
    \label{fig:ch5-ber_results}
\end{figure}

It is worth noting that for the DR4 transceiver, the measurements were performed using only one of 
its four spatial lanes. Unlike LWDM and CWDM, which multiplex four wavelengths into a single 
optical fiber, DR4 relies on parallel optics, utilizing four independent fibers that each carry a 
100G signal at 1310~nm. Given the single-input/single-output architecture of the photonic test 
structure, evaluating a single DR4 lane is mechanically necessary and fully representative of the 
standard's performance under DGD constraints.

For the strictly co-polarized injection states (pure TE and pure TM), the pre-FEC BER remains well 
below the standard KP4 FEC threshold (typically around $2.4 \times 10^{-4}$) 
\cite{ieee_802_3ck-2022} for all tested delay lines and transceiver formats. In these scenarios, 
the PSR routes almost the entirety of the optical power through a single physical arm. 
Consequently, the signal does not experience any multipath interference upon recombination at the 
PRC, making the transmission completely immune to the physical delay engineered in the parallel 
empty arm.

However, a critical degradation is observed for the 50-50\% TE-TM mixed state and the CWDM no-COP 
scenario. In these cases, the optical power is split between the upper and lower arms. 
At $\Delta L = 0~\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $\Delta L = 700~\mu\mathrm{m}$ (representing approximately 0 
and 10~ps of delay, respectively), the pre-FEC BER remains stable below the threshold, thus the 
FEC done by RS(544, 514) decoding ensures error-free transmission. This indicates that the internal 
digital signal processing (DSP) equalizers of the commercial 400G receivers are capable of 
successfully compensating for up to $\sim 10$~ps of temporal skew between the polarization 
branches.

Conversely, when the path length difference is increased to $3500~\mu\mathrm{m}$ 
($\sim 50$~ps) and $7000~\mu\mathrm{m}$ ($\sim 101$~ps), the link completely drops, marked as 
``FAIL'' in the corresponding plots. State-of-the-art 400G Ethernet relies on 53-Gbaud PAM4 
modulation, yielding a symbol duration of approximately 18.8~ps. A temporal skew of 50 or 100~ps 
means the delayed copy of the signal shifts by several symbol periods, causing severe inter-symbol 
interference (ISI) that vastly exceeds the equalization tap limits of the receiver DSP. This 
catastrophic failure is equally reflected in the CWDM no-COP measurements, as the independent and 
random polarization of each wavelength inherently forces power splitting across both delayed arms.

Ultimately, these dynamic results demonstrate that while the proposed polarization diversity scheme 
introduces negligible penalty under matched-length conditions, strict path length balancing is 
mandatory. To guarantee error-free 400G operation, the structural length difference between the 
active and passive arms must be carefully minimized to keep the temporal skew well within the 
receiver's equalization capabilities.

\section{Smart Transceiver in Next-Generation Switching Nodes}
\label{sec:switching_nodes}

\let\thefootnote\relax\footnotetext{\textit{The author would like to gratefully acknowledge 
Dr. José Manuel Rivas-Moscoso - Telefónica for providing the conceptual schematics of the switching 
node architectures discussed in this section.}}

As optical networks scale to accommodate the exponential growth in data traffic, the flexibility 
and reconfigurability of the routing elements become paramount. While the previous sections 
validated the performance of the proposed structures in point-to-point (P2P) links, modern optical 
networks rely heavily on dynamically reconfigurable optical switching nodes to manage traffic 
across meshed topologies. In this context, the integration of smart transceivers—capable of 
adapting to varying network conditions, routing paths, and signal formats—provides a significant 
advantage for next-generation architectures \cite{bhat6GEcosystemCurrent2021, 
welchPointtoMultipointOpticalNetworks2021}.

Optical switching nodes, often implemented as reconfigurable optical add-drop multiplexers 
(ROADMs), are typically based on two predominant internal architectures: broadcast-and-select 
(B\&S) and route-and-select (R\&S). In a broadcast-and-select architecture, such as the one 
depicted in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-broadcast_and_select}, incoming optical signals are passively 
split and distributed (broadcast) to all possible output directions using optical power splitters. 
In the architecture presented in the figure, the splitting is done exclusively by the programmable 
PIC core. At each output port, a wavelength selective switch (WSS) or a tunable filter is employed 
to select the specific wavelengths destined for that path, blocking all others. While B\&S is 
cost-effective and straightforward to implement for nodes with a low degree of connectivity, the 
inherent splitting losses scale poorly as the number of ports increases.

\begin{figure}[htpb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/ch5-broadcast_and_select.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of a broadcast-and-select (B\&S) optical switching node. 
    Courtesy of Dr. José Manuel Rivas-Moscoso - Telefónica. Note: PSR not shown for simplicity, but 
    two IO ports are required in the smart transceiver.}
    \label{fig:ch5-broadcast_and_select}
\end{figure}

Conversely, the route-and-select architecture utilizes active switching elements at both the 
ingress and egress stages, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:ch5-route_and_select}. For standard 
express traffic, an input WSS dynamically routes the incoming wavelengths directly to their 
intended output WSS, which then multiplexes them into the outgoing fiber. The R\&S paradigm 
significantly reduces the insertion losses associated with passive splitting, offering superior 
scalability and enhanced channel isolation.

In addition to point-to-point express routing, these nodes must support advanced multicast 
capabilities, such as the drop-and-continue (D\&C) functionality. D\&C allows a specific wavelength 
to be split within the node so that a fraction of the optical power is dropped locally to a 
receiver, while the remaining signal continues its path through the egress WSS toward subsequent 
nodes in the network. To enable this, the affected signals are diverted through an internal local 
mesh (typically comprising optical splitters or multicast switches). 

However, as indicated in the architectural schematics, express connections that do not require D\&C 
functionality can completely skip this internal mesh, traveling directly from the input WSS to the 
output WSS to minimize insertion losses and free up internal resources. Moreover, to provide 
further routing flexibility and enable contentionless add/drop capabilities, more than one 
connection can be established between each WSS and the local mesh, (if there are enough ports 
available).

\subsection{Smart Transceiver for Route \& Select Architecture: Performance Estimation and 
Limitations}

To evaluate the feasibility of deploying the proposed smart transceiver within a route-and-select 
architecture, a power budget analysis was conducted to estimate the total insertion losses and 
structural limitations. In this configuration, the optical signal traverses active switching 
components, such as the WSSs, and potentially the local mesh before interfacing with the 
transceiver. While the R\&S paradigm avoids the severe optical penalties associated with passive 
broadcasting, the cascading of optical switches intrinsically introduces cumulative insertion 
losses. 

Based on the transceiver's output power limits and the receiver's pre-FEC sensitivity threshold, 
the estimated total insertion losses for the R\&S optical path were calculated to be approximately 
[XX]~dB. Taking this available power margin into consideration, the maximum scalable dimensions of 
the switching elements were determined. The theoretical analysis reveals that the system power 
budget can reliably support a maximum of [YY] cascaded stages when using $1\times 2$ switching 
configurations. Furthermore, if higher-radix switches are employed to minimize the footprint, the 
transceiver can sustain up to [ZZ] cascaded stages of $1\times 4$ configurations without requiring 
intermediate optical amplification.

\subsection{Smart Transceiver for Broadcast \& Select Architecture: Performance Estimation and 
Limitations}

In the broadcast-and-select architecture, the power budget constraints are dictated by 
fundamentally different mechanisms. As highlighted in the architectural schematics, a distinctive 
advantage of integrating the proposed smart transceiver is that the optical splitting required for 
the broadcast operation can be performed exclusively by the programmable PIC core, eliminating the 
need for external passive splitters. 

However, this inherent power division inside the chip still imposes a strict physical limit. In 
ideal conditions, every $1\times 2$ optical split intrinsically reduces the signal power by at 
least 3~dB, in addition to the excess insertion and propagation losses of the on-chip tunable 
couplers and waveguides. For this scenario, the theoretical calculations yield an estimated total 
insertion loss of approximately [AA]~dB. Because the splitting penalty scales logarithmically with 
the node degree, the available optical power budget directly limits the maximum connectivity of the 
node. The analysis demonstrates that, relying solely on the smart transceiver's capabilities, the 
maximum number of reachable directions—or adjacent nodes—is limited to [BB]. Beyond this threshold, 
the split optical signal falls below the detectable sensitivity limit, meaning that further scaling 
of the B\&S node would strictly require optical amplification.
