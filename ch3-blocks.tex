\chapter{Optimization and Characterization of Fundamental SOI Building Blocks}\label{chap:blocks} % (fold)

\section{Introduction to the Fabrication Platform} \label{sec:fabrication_platform}

Developing the programmable photonic circuits discussed in this thesis depends fundamentally on the 
maturity and precision of the Silicon-on-Insulator platform. Outlined in this section are the 
technological framework, the computational tools employed for device optimization, and the 
experimental infrastructure used to validate the performance of these fundamental building blocks.

\subsection{SOI Fabrication Platforms} \label{subsec:foundry}

The devices characterized in this work were fabricated using dedicated wafer runs across different 
fabrication facilities. The use of accumulated dedicated runs allowed for full-wafer layout control 
and the implementation of specific process modelling. This is essential for design optimizations 
tailored to the requirements of large-scale programmable architectures. 

The baseline platform consists of a standard SOI wafer with a nominal silicon device layer 
thickness of $220$~nm, resting on a thick Buried Oxide (BOX) layer that ensures modal isolation 
from the handle substrate. To ensure portability across different manufacturing environments, the 
building blocks were adapted to the specific Process Design Kits (PDKs) and design rules of each 
foundry. This included adjustments in the slab thickness for rib geometries (typically ranging 
between $70$~nm and $90$~nm) and the re-optimization of critical dimensions to comply with 
fabrication constraints, such as the minimum achievable tip width for the silicon waveguides.

Furthermore, the designs account for the specific Back-End-Of-Line (BEOL) stack of each process. 
The vertical distance and composition of the dielectric cladding and metal layers were critical 
parameters for the optimization of active components, such as thermo-optic phase shifters, ensuring 
that the building blocks remain compatible with the multi-layer metal routing required for 
large-scale system integration.

\subsection{Design and Simulation Workflow} \label{subsec:workflow}

Transitioning from theoretical concepts to physical layout follows a rigorous multi-step simulation 
and optimization workflow. The choice of numerical method was strategically dictated by the 
physical dimensions and the nature of the light-matter interaction in each building block to 
balance computational efficiency and accuracy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Waveguide and Modal Analysis:} The fundamental propagation properties, including 
    effective indices ($n_{eff}$) and group indices ($n_g$), were analyzed using Finite Difference 
    Eigenmode (FDE) solvers. This analysis provided the necessary baseline for phase shifter 
    designs and established the confinement conditions across the different SOI platforms used.
    
    \item \textbf{Edge Coupler Simulation (BPM):} Given the relatively large longitudinal 
    dimensions of the spot-size converters---typically in the order of hundreds of 
    micrometers---the Beam Propagation Method (BPM) was employed. Specifically, Synopsys RSoft 
    BeamPROP was used to simulate the adiabatic expansion of the mode along the inverted tapers. 
    This approach allowed for an efficient evaluation of coupling efficiency and modal evolution, 
    providing a reliable design framework with a significantly lower computational cost than 
    full-wave methods.
    
    \item \textbf{MMI Optimization (FDTD):} For the design of compact and high-performance MMI 
    couplers, where scattering and multi-mode interference effects are dominant, the 
    Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) method was utilized via Ansys Lumerical. This solver 
    provides the necessary precision to capture broadband responses and complex phase 
    relationships. Furthermore, the Adjoint optimization method was integrated into the FDTD 
    workflow to explore a high-dimensional design space, resulting in the optimized geometries 
    discussed in Section \ref{sec:mmi_adjoint}.
    
    \item \textbf{Layout Generation:} Once the design targets were achieved, the device geometries 
    were translated into GDSII files. Depending on the specific fabrication run and circuit 
    complexity, layouts were generated using Synopsys OptoDesigner for certain designs, while the 
    Python-based framework gdsfactory was utilized for others. These tools enabled script-based 
    generation to ensure sub-nanometer precision in the definition of adiabatic curves and complex 
    optimized shapes, with KLayout serving as the primary environment for final inspection and 
    design rule verification.
\end{itemize}

Other specialized components, such as Arrayed Waveguide Gratings (AWGs), required dedicated 
simulation approaches tailored to their specific dispersive properties. For these devices, the 
EPIPProp tool from Photon Design was employed, which provides a specialized framework for modeling 
the complex phase and amplitude relationships in waveguide-array-based structures. Further details 
on these designs will be provided in their respective sections later in this work.

To validate the performance of the designed building blocks, two distinct experimental 
characterization benches were employed. Both setups share a common optical backbone consisting of a 
tunable CW laser source, a polarization controller to excite the fundamental TE mode, and 
high-sensitivity power meters. The choice between these setups depends on whether the 
characterization requires high flexibility for active tuning or statistical data through 
high-throughput measurements.

For the precise extraction of performance metrics in passive components, such as the excess loss 
and imbalance of MMI couplers, a high-throughput setup is utilized. As shown in the schematic of 
Figure \ref{fig:ch3-hexapod_setup}a, this bench is equipped with a 64-channel fiber array mounted

\cite{chrostowskiSiliconPhotonicsDesign2015, zhenshengCompactLowLossMMI2012}. By measuring chains 
of 16 to 18 cascaded MMIs, the cumulative loss can be determined with significantly reduced 
experimental error. This statistical approach allows for the extraction of the mean insertion loss 
per component through linear regression of the cascaded stages, effectively filtering out 
fluctuations from single-device measurements and fiber-to-chip coupling variations.

\subsubsection{Thermal Management and Stability}

Given the high thermo-optic coefficient of silicon \cite{cocorullo2002measurement}, all 
measurements were conducted under strict thermal control to prevent unwanted spectral shifts and 
ensure the reliability of the experimental data. The photonic chips were mounted on a specialized 
vacuum chuck equipped with a high-power Thermo-Electric Cooler (TEC) and a feedback thermistor. 
This closed-loop system ensures a constant operating temperature, typically maintained at 
$25^{\circ}\text{C}$ or $45^{\circ}\text{C}$. This stabilization is critical to avoid the drift of 
resonant structures and to ensure the repeatability of the results across different fabrication 
runs and measurement sessions.


\section{Simple Inverted Taper for Edge Coupling} \label{sec:taper}

While grating couplers offer high flexibility for wafer-level testing, they present significant 
limitations for certain advanced packaging paradigms. Specifically, the vertical emission of 
grating couplers is often incompatible with flip-chip bonding architectures, where the top 
surface of the photonic chip is obstructed by a mounted electronic or laser die. To overcome this 
and enable the ultra-dense integration required for the systems described in Chapter 
\ref{chap:systems}, a side-coupling approach using a simple inverted taper was implemented.


\subsection{Design Rationale and Geometry}

The edge coupler design is dictated by the specific layer stack and the lithographic constraints of 
the SOI foundry used in this work. Figure \ref{fig:ch3-taper_design}(a) illustrates the particular
cross-section of the platform, while Figure \ref{fig:ch3-taper_design}(b) presents the top-view 
geometry of the implemented inverted taper.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/ch3-edge_coupler_design.png}
    \caption{Geometry of the inverted taper edge coupler: (a) Cross-section of the SOI stack 
    provided by the foundry. (b) Top-view of the $500 \ \mu\text{m}$ long linear taper 
    transitioning from the bus waveguide to the $100 \text{ nm}$ tip. Note: Dimensions in (b) are 
    not to scale.}
    \label{fig:ch3-taper_design}
\end{figure}

To maximize mode expansion at the chip facet, physical dimensions for the SSC were selected 
according to these foundry-specific parameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Waveguide Stack:} The $220 \text{ nm}$ thick silicon core sits on a $2200 
    \text{ nm}$ BOX and is covered by a $4000 \text{ nm}$ $SiO_2$ upper cladding. This stack 
    thickness is critical as it defines the maximum volume available for the mode to expand as it 
    delocalizes from the silicon core.
    \item \textbf{Lithographic Limit ($w_{tip}$):} Width reduction from the nominal 
    $500 \text{ nm}$ to a tip width of $100 \text{ nm}$ was implemented. As the minimum feature 
    size allowed by the foundry's design rules, this tip represents the narrowest possible geometry 
    to achieve the highest MFD mismatch reduction.
    \item \textbf{Adiabatic Transition ($L_{taper}$):} A length of $500 \ \mu\text{m}$ was
    implemented to ensure an adiabatic modal transition. This length ensures that power is 
    transferred efficiently from the confined waveguide mode to the expanded cladding mode without 
    significant radiation losses.
\end{itemize}

As the device length is three orders of magnitude larger than its width, the schematic in 
Figure \ref{fig:ch3-taper_design}(b) uses a modified aspect ratio for clarity, focusing on the 
geometric transition rather than physical proportions.



\subsection{Simulation and Performance Baseline}

Beam Propagation Method (BPM) simulations conducted in RSoft established a performance baseline for 
the design. Given that the focus of this work lies in the system-level integration rather 
than the optimization of the coupling interface itself, a straightforward linear geometry was 
prioritized for its fabrication robustness.
The simulation also pretends verifying the assumption that standard optical fibers are 
fundamentally mismatched with the mode expansion capabilities of a $100 \text{ nm}$ silicon tip. 
By establishing a performance baseline, we evaluate the necessity of employing specialized 
high-numerical aperture fibers and quantify the impact of fabrication tolerances.
Figure \ref{fig:ch3-taper_sims} summarizes the performance of the 
inverted taper regarding modal matching, spectral behavior, and dicing tolerances.

Analyzing the relationship between fiber MFD and coupling efficiency in Figure 
\ref{fig:ch3-taper_sims}(a) confirms that the delocalized mode at the $100 \text{ nm}$ tip is 
insufficient to match the $\sim 10.4 \ \mu\text{m}$ MFD of a standard SMF-28 fiber, resulting in 
losses above $4.25 \text{ dB}$. To validate the need for a specialized interface, we compared these 
results with Ultra-High Numerical Aperture (UHNA) fibers. As illustrated in the 
spectral response in Figure \ref{fig:ch3-taper_sims}(b), UHNA1 \cite{coherent_uhna1} 
($MFD \approx 4.8 \ \mu\text{m}$) provides the optimal match with an excess loss of approximately 
$2.5 \text{ dB}$ and a remarkably flat behavior across the C-band. In contrast, UHNA7 
\cite{coherent_uhna7} ($MFD \approx 3.2 \ \mu\text{m}$) shows higher losses due to 
over-confinement, confirming UHNA1 as the necessary bridge for this SOI interface.

increase in loss with a slope of approximately $0.1 \text{ dB}/\mu\text{m}$. This sensitivity 
analysis is critical for the packaging stages described in Chapter \ref{chap:systems}, as it 
quantifies the power budget penalty associated with facet preparation tolerances.

The experimental results presented in Table \ref{tab:ec_combined_results} lead to several key 
conclusions regarding the edge coupler performance:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Validation of the Simulation Model:} The best-performing devices 
    (EC\#7 and EC\#8) achieved insertion losses of approximately $8.33~\text{dB}$ 
    ($4.16~\text{dB/facet}$), which is in excellent agreement with the $4.40~\text{dB/facet}$ 
    predicted by simulations.
    \item \textbf{Efficiency and Lithographic Trade-off:} The absolute coupling losses are 
    acknowledged to be higher than those of record-breaking State-of-the-Art (SOTA) designs. This 
    is a direct consequence of the $100~\text{nm}$ taper tip limitation, representing a deliberate 
    trade-off where record efficiency was secondary to Design for Manufacturability (DfM) and 
    high-yield fabrication.
    \item \textbf{Fault-Tolerance to Dicing Inaccuracies:} The results confirm the primary thesis 
    objective: the designs with a $10~\mu\text{m}$ retraction (EC\#6--8) showed equivalent or 
    superior performance to the nominal design. This demonstrates that the inverted taper is highly 
    resilient to dicing offsets, validating its fault-tolerant nature.
    \item \textbf{Adiabaticity and Footprint:} The results for EC\#1, the shorter variant, which 
    achieved $9.10~\text{dB}$ of total loss, demonstrate that reducing the taper length does not 
    lead to a significant penalty in coupling efficiency. This confirms that the taper profile 
    remains largely adiabatic even in its more compact version, offering a potential path for 
    reducing the total footprint of the PIC without compromising performance.
\end{itemize}


In terms of absolute performance, it must be acknowledged that the measured coupling 
losses—averaging $4.16\text{--}4.55~\text{dB/facet}$—are relatively high compared to 
state-of-the-art edge couplers reported in the literature, where values below $1.5~\text{dB}$ or 
even sub-decibel performance are common \cite{barwiczAutomatedHighthroughputPhotonic2018, 
papesFiberchipEdgeCoupler2016, yiPolarizationinsensitiveLowlossOband2025, 
gaoEfficientSiliconphotonicsEdge2025}. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to the lithographic 
constraints of the dedicated fabrication runs, which limited the minimum taper tip width to 
$100~\text{nm}$. As demonstrated in the simulation analysis, this tip width prevents a more 
effective mode expansion, leading to a significant mode mismatch with the high-NA fiber.

However, the primary objective of this design was not to achieve record-breaking efficiency, but 
rather to demonstrate a fault-tolerant interface that enables high-density integration systems, 
as discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:systems}. In this regard, the designed fab-tolerant coupler 
maintains a consistent and predictable performance despite significant dicing misalignments of up 
to $10~\mu\text{m}$. For applications where assembly reliability and tolerance to mechanical 
inaccuracies are prioritized over a strict power budget—such as the fault-tolerant PIC architecture 
proposed in this work—this trade-off between absolute loss and fabrication robustness is justified.

In conclusion, the edge coupler has been successfully validated as a reliable and resilient entry 
point for the optical signal. Having established this stable fiber-to-chip interface, the following 
section describes the design and characterization of the MMI couplers used for on-chip power 
splitting.


\section{High-Performance MMI via Adjoint Optimization}  

\label{sec:mmi_adjoint}

The $2\times2$ Multimode Interference (MMI) coupler is the fundamental building block for the 
splitting and cross-coupling of optical signals within the system. As established in 
Section~\ref{sec:ch2-puc}, each PUC is based on a symmetric MZI architecture, which inherently 
incorporates two MMIs in its structure. Consequently, the insertion loss of an individual coupler 
is encountered twice within every unit cell, compounding the total power penalty across the 
programmable fabric.

In fact, these couplers are identified as the predominant source of loss within the integrated 
architecture. Therefore, reducing splitter losses is not merely a design refinement but a critical 
requirement for the scalability of the system; minimizing the power penalty at each junction is 
essential to preserve a viable power budget as the number of cascaded PUCs increases. This section 
details the experimental validation of our baseline design and the subsequent implementation of 
advanced shape optimization techniques to push its performance toward the physical limit.

\subsection{Parametric Baseline Design}\label{subsec:baseline_mmi}

The design process for the MMI stage originated from a $2\times2$ geometry previously optimized 
through conventional parametric sweeps following the self-imaging 
principle~\cite{soldanoOpticalMultimodeInterference1995}.  

To maximize modal control, a multi-level shallow-etch process was implemented, as illustrated in 
the cross-section of Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-mmi_geometry}(a). The MMI body features a total height of 
$220~\text{nm} (h_{220})$ and is etched down to a $150~\text{nm}$ silicon slab ($h_{150}$), 
providing high confinement for the interference pattern. As shown in the top-view in 
Figure~\ref{fig:ch3-mmi_geometry}(b), this interference region has a footprint of 
$33.7~\mu\text{m} \times 5~\mu\text{m}$ and is placed upon a $15~\mu\text{m}$ wide slab to prevent 
boundary scattering. Furthermore, the access ports include $3~\mu\text{m}$ long tapers that utilize 
a deeper $90~\text{nm}$ etch ($h_{90}$) to bridge the modal mismatch and ensure a smooth transition 
from the access waveguides.

Despite these high-performance metrics, this rectangular geometry represents a performance 
plateau for traditional design methods. The following subsections describe the methodology used to 
push these boundaries even further, employing shape optimization to minimize the remaining 
scattering and further flatten the spectral response.


\subsection{Shape Optimization Methodology}

To determine if the performance of the experimentally-validated $2\times2$ MMI could be further 
enhanced, a shape optimization strategy was implemented using the \texttt{lumopt} framework within 
the Ansys Lumerical suite. The core objective was to move beyond the limitations of rigid 
rectangular boundaries and allow the device walls to evolve into an organic profile that better 
matches the internal multimode interference patterns, thereby suppressing scattering and 
mode-mismatch losses.

\subsubsection{FDTD Simulation Setup and Computational Domain}

Constructing the optimization environment relied on a custom Python-driven \texttt{build} script 
designed to replicate the multi-level geometry described in Section~\ref{subsec:baseline_mmi}. 
This script ensures that the complex etch profiles ($h_{220}$, $h_{150}$, and $h_{90}$) are 
precisely mapped into the 3D-FDTD domain. By parameterizing the baseline footprint and its 
surrounding slab region within the \texttt{lumopt} framework, the environment is prepared to handle 
the subsequent morphological changes of the side-walls while maintaining the physical constraints 
of the SOI platform.

To guarantee high gradient accuracy, the numerical simulation was configured with the following 
parameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Symmetry and Boundary Conditions:} A 3D-FDTD region was defined using Z-symmetry 
    at the center of the silicon layer. This optimization reduces the computational overhead by 
    half without compromising the accuracy of the fundamental TE mode analysis.
    \item \textbf{Mesh Resolution:} A mesh step of $\Delta x = \Delta y = \Delta z = 45~\text{nm}$ 
    (Mesh Accuracy 4) was utilized to balance simulation time and accuracy during the iterative 
    process. 
    \item \textbf{Spectral Window:} The simulation targets the extended C-band 
    ($1520\text{--}1580~\text{nm}$) using $11$ frequency points. This $60~\text{nm}$ bandwidth 
    aligns with recent industry trends toward \textit{Super C-band} systems that aim to maximize 
    fiber capacity.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Inverse Design Framework and Figure of Merit}

The optimization process is based on the gradient-based Adjoint 
Method~\cite{lalau-keralyShapeOptimizationNanophotonic2014}. This inverse-design technique is 
particularly efficient as it calculates the sensitivity of a defined Figure of Merit (FoM) with 
respect to an arbitrary number of design parameters using only two full-wave simulations per 
iteration: a forward and an adjoint simulation~\cite{piggottInverseDesignDemonstration2015}. 
The optimization was performed using the \texttt{lumopt} framework, employing the L-BFGS-B 
algorithm for efficient convergence under boundary constraints~\cite{byrdLimitedMemoryAlgorithm1995}.


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Geometry Parameterization:} A custom \texttt{ParameterizedGeometry} 
    function was used to define the side-walls. The boundary was constructed using cubic spline 
    interpolation (\texttt{interp1d} with \textit{cubic} kind) based on a set of lateral 
    displacement parameters. This approach ensures a smooth, continuous curvature that is more 
    physically realistic and lower-loss than a discrete vertex-based polygon.
    \item \textbf{Symmetry Enforcement:} To ensure a consistent $50/50$ splitting ratio and 
    preserve the phase relationship, a four-fold symmetry was enforced. The algorithm optimizes the 
    parameters of a single quadrant, which is then reflected across both the longitudinal and 
    transversal axes to build the full MMI geometry.
    \item \textbf{Figure of Merit (FoM):} A multi-objective FoM was defined by summing the 
    mode-match transmission of both output ports (\texttt{fomS31} and \texttt{fomS41}). The target 
    was set to $0.5$ for each port across the entire $60~\text{nm}$ bandwidth, effectively 
    minimizing both insertion loss and imbalance.
    \item \textbf{Constraints:} Lateral boundaries were constrained within a range of 
    $\pm 200~\text{nm}$ (\textit{d\_bound}) relative to the initial width to maintain the device 
    within a manufacturable footprint and ensure the stability of the interference pattern.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Computational Workflow and Optimization Stages}

The optimization was carried out in two main stages, moving from a constrained body-only refinement 
to a comprehensive device optimization. This phased approach allowed for a systematic evaluation of 
the impact of numerical resolution and fabrication constraints.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1: Body-Only Optimization:} In this stage, only the side-walls of the MMI 
    interference region were optimized, while the access tapers remained fixed to the baseline 
    geometry. To identify the most reliable and high-performance configuration, four distinct cases 
    were evaluated:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Etch depth comparison:} Two scenarios were tested based on the taper etch 
        levels: $h_{90}$ (deep etch) and $h_{150}$ (shallow etch).
        \item \textbf{Numerical convergence:} For each etch depth, the optimization was performed 
        at both low resolution (for rapid design space exploration) and high resolution (to ensure 
        gradient accuracy and eliminate mesh artifacts).
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Stage 2: Integrated Body and Access Optimization:} Leveraging the experience and 
    results from the first stage, the optimization was extended to include both the MMI body and 
    the access tapers. Based on the convergence study in Stage 1, this final refinement was 
    performed exclusively using the optimal etch-depth configuration and high-resolution settings 
    to achieve the physical limit of the device performance.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Stage 2: Integrated Device Optimization and Final Performance}

the 90 nm etch depth configuration, which was identified in Stage 1 as the most promising 
architecture for achieving high power symmetry.

stability. Although Stage 1 established that high-resolution meshes provide superior absolute

L-BFGS-B optimizer to identify the optimal morphological trends without the numerical noise often 
present in high-dimensional adjoint sensitivities.

\subsubsection{Experimental Characterization and Validation}

To validate the theoretical performance of the optimized architectures, a total of eight 
independent structures were selected for fabrication on the SOI platform. These devices are 
classified into three groups according to the optimization stage and the specific parameters 
evaluated:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Body Optimizations (Stage 1):} Devices \textbf{D1} and \textbf{D2} represent the 
    optimized MMI bodies without integrated taper refinement. \textbf{D1} utilizes the 150 nm 
    shallow etch layer, while \textbf{D2} utilizes the 90 nm layer. Both were optimized using 
    high-resolution meshes ($45 \times 10^{-6}$ mesh size and accuracy 4).
    \item \textbf{Integrated Body + Access Optimizations (Stage 2):} Devices \textbf{D3} and 
    \textbf{D4} represent the holistic design approach. Both utilize the 90 nm shallow layer. 
    \textbf{D3} serves as the low-resolution benchmark ($60 \times 10^{-6}$ mesh, accuracy 1), 
    whereas \textbf{D4} is the final optimized design at high-resolution ($45 \times 10^{-6}$ mesh, 
    accuracy 4).
    \item \textbf{Tolerance and Sensitivity Study (D4 Variants):} To assess the robustness of the 
    final integrated design (\textbf{D4}), four variants were fabricated to simulate critical 
    manufacturing deviations:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{D4.1 and D4.2:} Evaluate longitudinal tolerances by increasing and decreasing 
        the MMI body length by 0.25 $\mu$m, respectively.
        \item \textbf{D4.3 and D4.4:} Evaluate transversal tolerances by increasing and decreasing 
        the MMI body width by 0.25 $\mu$m, respectively.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusions}

The development and characterization of the 3\,dB power splitters presented in this section 
demonstrate that a hierarchical, adjoint-based optimization strategy could be a powerful solution
for achieving high-performance photonic components. The transition from theoretical design to 
experimental validation yielded several key findings:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optimization Methodology:} The two-stage approach successfully identified the 
    90\,nm shallow etch as the optimal depth for power balance. The subsequent 45-parameter 
    integrated refinement in Stage 2 proved robust, producing an organic MMI morphology that 
    maintains high performance across the entire C-band.
    
    \item \textbf{Experimental Validation and CD Bias:} While the nominal designs showed a 
    systematic power imbalance due to foundry-specific fabrication offsets, the inclusion of 
    geometric variations allowed for a precise calibration of the process. Design \textbf{D4.4}, 
    featuring a $-0.25\,\mu$m width compensation, effectively neutralized the positive CD bias of 
    the fabrication run, achieving a near-ideal $50/50$ splitting ratio.
    
    \item \textbf{Performance Excellence:} Experimental results for the optimized \textbf{D4.4} 
    design indicate an exceptional peak Excess Loss of approximately $0.06$\,dB at $1550$\,nm, 
    with losses remaining below $0.15$\,dB across the $1520$--$1580$\,nm range. This matches the 
    high-fidelity numerical predictions and confirms the efficiency of the adjoint-optimized 
    geometry.
    
    \item \textbf{Numerical and Statistical Robustness:} The use of mid-resolution meshes during 
    the adjoint process ensured algorithm stability without sacrificing physical accuracy. 
    Furthermore, the narrow interquartile range observed in the experimental data across multiple 
    dies validates the high tolerance of the proposed MMI structure to standard wafer-scale process 
    variations.
\end{itemize}

Ultimately, the successful experimental validation of these splitters—achieving both low loss and 
perfect power balance through geometric calibration—provides a reliable and high-performance 
building block for the complex wideband photonic integrated circuits discussed in the subsequent 
chapters.
